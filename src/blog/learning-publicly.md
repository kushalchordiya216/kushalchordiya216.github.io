Frontend. Backend. Infrastructure. Security.

Long ago, the four disciplines lived together in harmony. Then, everything changed when the agents attacked.
When the industry needed developers most, it felt like we all just... vanished. Or at least, our courage did.

Around December 2025, the agentic harnesses seemed to have broken through a previously unseen barrier. It started with Claude Code and Opus 4.6. Suddenly, the "AI assistants" weren't incompetent and didn't need hand-holding. I personally went from being supremely frustrated with the clanker generated slop that other's on my team kept pushing to feeling genuine shock & awe at claude code's ability to one-shot things that would have taken me weeks.

People have been dooming about AI for years. I still remember the collective heart attack when GitHub Copilot first dropped and we all thought it would take our jobs (lol, lmao even). But the vibe has shifted now. There’s real fear in the air. Every time Claude one-shots a feature, 10 more CS grads enroll in a plumbing apprenticeship.

And I’m not immune to the dread. I feel it too.


![I'm tired boss](/assets/images/im_tired_boss.jpg)

That’s why I’m starting this. I’m taking all that "build in public" advice people keep shouting and actually doing it. This is my public record of trying to keep up. I'm going to take the sage advice of people far more accomplished than me and ask myself: "What did I get done this week?"
Every single week, I’m going to learn something, ship something, and write about it. 
It might something advanced like a learning how flash attention works and writing a very simple nanogpt style implementation or it might be a something as basic as understanding how the ls command actually works (seriously, you use it everyday, how many of you know how it actually works?) 

But, I don’t want to just build toy projects that rot in a repo. I want to create things that people can actually use. If I'm going to spend my time on this, it might as well be useful to someone else. Through this journey, I’m hoping to discover what the future of software as a profession actually looks like, for us normal folk. I want to find the tools that matter and the workflows that won't be obsolete by next Tuesday. As I figure it out, I’ll be documenting the learnings and the advice. If you're stressed and worried about your career, consider this my attempt to build a playbook we can all use.

There’s a lot of talk about AI replacing our jobs. But I don't think engaging in pessimism or worrying about job security helps anyone. There's no way to know if these changes will be a net positive for us as software engineers, so the only way forward is to assume they will be. We have to proceed with the assumption that AI will be a benefit, and the only question left to answer is how we draw out that benefit. Honestly, we have to assume things will work out, because there's simply no other way to live life. If ideas are cheap and execution is cheap and intelligence is cheap, then the only thing you have left is your courage. So, now more than ever, you must be courageous, you must be a relentless optimist. As the big man says:

> "Pessimists tend to be right, but optimists tend to succeed" - Mark Zuckerberg

So, that's it for the first entry. Let's see where it goes from here.
What about you? What did you get done this week?
